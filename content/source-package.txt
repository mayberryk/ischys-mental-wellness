Can Caffeine Make Anxiety or ADHD Symptoms Worse?
URL: /learning/caffeine-anxiety-adhd/
Primary search question: Can caffeine worsen anxiety, sleep, heart rate, or stimulant side effects?
Opening answer: It can for some people. Caffeine sensitivity varies, but higher intake can contribute to jitteriness, anxiety-like sensations, sleep disruption, palpitations, and blood-pressure changes. Total stimulant exposure matters when caffeine is combined with ADHD medication.

Hobbies That Support Mental Health: Finding Something That Helps Your Brain Recharge
Mental wellness isn't only about medication or therapy. What you repeatedly do with your time
can influence stress, mood, attention, sleep, confidence, and your sense of connection with other
people.
A hobby won't replace treatment for a mental health condition, but meaningful activities can
become an important part of taking care of your brain.
The best hobby isn't necessarily the one that's supposed to be "good for mental health." It's one
you actually enjoy enough to keep doing.
Why Can Hobbies Help?
When we're depressed, anxious, overwhelmed, or burned out, life can gradually become smaller.
We may stop doing things that previously gave us pleasure, accomplishment, connection, or
purpose.
That can create a cycle:
Feeling poorly doing less experiencing fewer rewarding moments feeling even worse. → → →
Psychotherapy sometimes addresses this through behavioral activation—intentionally bringing
meaningful and rewarding activities back into your life rather than waiting until you feel
motivated enough to start.
Different hobbies can support mental health in different ways. Some provide physical activity.
Others promote creativity, mastery, mindfulness, social connection, or simply a break from
repetitive thinking.
Here are some possibilities.
1. Music
Play an instrument, sing, join a choir, make playlists, attend concerts, or simply listen
intentionally.
Music can influence arousal and emotion, making it useful both when you need to settle your
nervous system and when you need energy.
You don't need musical talent to benefit from music. The goal isn't performance—it's engagement.
Try: Create three playlists: one for calming down, one for getting moving, and one containing
songs that reliably improve your mood.
2. Get Outside
Walking, hiking, fishing, birdwatching, camping, or simply sitting outside can provide something
many of us don't get enough of: movement, daylight, sensory stimulation, and distance from
screens and everyday demands.
You don't need to climb a mountain.
A walk through a park counts.
Try: Put your phone away for 10–20 minutes and deliberately notice what you can see, hear,
smell, and feel around you.
3. Gardening and Growing Things
Gardening combines several potentially beneficial ingredients: movement, sensory experiences,
routine, nurturing something over time, and visible evidence of progress.
No yard? Try herbs in the kitchen, a few houseplants, or a community garden.
There is something psychologically powerful about caring for something today that you won't see
the full results of until later.
4. Cooking and Baking
Cooking provides creativity, planning, sensory stimulation, and a tangible reward at the end.
It can also become a social activity. Cook with a partner, teach a child a family recipe, invite
someone to dinner, or learn dishes from another culture.
If cooking feels like another chore, however, don't force it into becoming your "self-care." Choose
something you genuinely enjoy.
5. Dance and Movement
Movement doesn't have to mean going to the gym.
Dancing, yoga, swimming, cycling, martial arts, pickleball, running, walking, or recreational
sports can combine physical activity with enjoyment.
Dance has an additional advantage: music, movement, coordination, learning, and sometimes
social connection happen simultaneously.
And dancing badly in your kitchen still counts.
6. Make Something
Painting, drawing, woodworking, pottery, knitting, crocheting, sewing, jewelry making, model
building, scrapbooking, or other hands-on projects can give your attention somewhere productive
to go.
Creative hobbies can be especially valuable for people whose minds rarely seem to turn off.
The point isn't whether the finished product is "good."
Creating and performing are two different things. You are allowed to create something
without being good at it.
7. Photography
Photography can change the way you pay attention.
Instead of moving automatically through your environment, you begin looking for light, color,
expressions, textures, landscapes, and small details you might otherwise miss.
Try a simple challenge: photograph one interesting or beautiful thing every day for a month.
Your phone camera is enough.
8. Writing and Journaling
Writing can help organize experiences that feel chaotic when they're only circulating in your

Traditional journaling isn't for everyone. Other options include:
gratitude journaling
poetry
fiction
songwriting
letters you never send
recording funny observations
keeping a travel journal
writing down three things that went well that day
You don't need to produce pages. Five minutes can be enough.
9. Mind-Body Activities
Yoga, tai chi, qigong, stretching, breathing exercises, and meditation combine attention with
awareness of the body.
For some people, sitting still and trying to "clear the mind" is frustrating—particularly when they
have ADHD, significant anxiety, or racing thoughts.
Movement-based mindfulness may be easier.
The goal isn't to have no thoughts. It's practicing the ability to notice where your attention has
gone and gently redirect it.
10. Do Something With Other People
Sometimes the most therapeutic ingredient isn't the hobby itself—it's connection.
Consider a recreational sports league, running or walking group, choir, book club, gaming group,
volunteer organization, church/community activity, crafting group, or class.
This can be particularly useful when depression or anxiety has gradually led to isolation.
You don't have to become highly social. Even regularly being around people who share an interest
can create a sense of belonging.

This is important.

That creates a frustrating problem: people are often told to "do something you enjoy" precisely

Instead of asking:
"What sounds fun?"

Small actions still count.

Instead of looking for the single "best" hobby for mental health, think about what may be missing
from your life right now.
If you're isolated: choose something involving people.
If you're mentally overstimulated: try nature, gardening, repetitive crafts, or gentle movement.
If you're bored or under-stimulated: consider music, dance, sports, learning, or something
challenging.
If you're feeling ineffective or discouraged: choose something that produces visible progress or
a finished product.
If you're spending too much time in your head: consider a physical or hands-on activity.
If life feels like nothing but responsibilities: choose something with absolutely no productive
purpose other than enjoyment.
That last category matters.
You don't have to monetize your hobby, become exceptional at it, post it online, or turn it into
another accomplishment.
Sometimes play is enough.

Caffeine: More Than Just a Morning Pick-Me-Up
Coffee, tea, soda, energy drinks, pre-workout supplements, chocolate, and even some medications
can contain caffeine.
For many people, moderate caffeine use causes few problems. But caffeine is still a psychoactive
stimulant, which means it affects your brain, nervous system, sleep, and cardiovascular system.
When we're trying to understand your mental health—or determine whether a medication is
working—your caffeine intake can be an important piece of the puzzle.
What Does Caffeine Do?
Caffeine primarily works by blocking adenosine, a chemical that builds up throughout the day
and helps your brain recognize that it is becoming tired.
Blocking adenosine can temporarily make you feel:
More awake
More alert
More energetic
Better able to concentrate
Less fatigued
But more isn't necessarily better.
Higher amounts can contribute to:
Jitteriness or shakiness
Racing heart or palpitations
Increased blood pressure
Anxiety or panic-like sensations
Irritability
Restlessness
Headaches
Stomach upset
Difficulty falling or staying asleep
People differ considerably in their sensitivity to caffeine.
Caffeine + ADHD
There's a reason some people with ADHD are drawn to coffee, soda, or energy drinks: caffeine is
stimulating.
Some people report temporarily feeling more alert or focused after caffeine. That doesn't mean
caffeine treats ADHD as reliably as established ADHD treatments.
The bigger concern is total stimulant load.
If you take a stimulant medication and also consume significant amounts of caffeine, you may be
more likely to experience:
racing heart • elevated blood pressure • jitteriness • anxiety • irritability • tremor • appetite
suppression • difficulty sleeping
Sometimes a patient believes their medication dose is too high when caffeine is contributing to
the symptoms.
Other times, large amounts of caffeine are being used to compensate for inadequate sleep,
untreated symptoms, or medication wearing off.
That's why it's important for your psychiatric provider to know how much caffeine you're actually
consuming.
Caffeine, Anxiety & Panic
Caffeine can create physical sensations that resemble anxiety:
faster heartbeat • sweating • trembling • restlessness • rapid breathing • feeling "wired"
For someone prone to panic attacks, those physical sensations can sometimes become the
beginning of a panic cycle.
If anxiety suddenly becomes worse, caffeine is one of several factors worth examining.
Caffeine, Depression & Fatigue
When you're exhausted or depressed, caffeine can feel like a quick solution.
The problem can become a cycle:
Poor sleep fatigue more caffeine poorer sleep more fatigue more caffeine → → → → →
Rather than simply adding caffeine, persistent fatigue deserves investigation.
Sleep quality, depression, medications, iron deficiency, thyroid problems, sleep apnea, nutrition,
and other medical conditions can all contribute to fatigue.
Caffeine & Recovery From Substance Use
Caffeine doesn't need to be automatically eliminated during recovery.
However, it's worth paying attention to how it is being used.
For some people, heavy caffeine or energy-drink consumption becomes another way of
repeatedly changing how they feel—particularly when boredom, fatigue, stress, cravings, or
emotional discomfort appear.
That's something to become curious about rather than ashamed of.
Ask yourself:
Am I drinking this because I enjoy it—or because I feel like I can't function without it?
Caffeine Can Affect Your Heart Rate and Blood Pressure
Caffeine can temporarily increase heart rate or blood pressure in some people.
This becomes especially relevant when we are also prescribing medications that can affect
cardiovascular function.
If we're monitoring your blood pressure or pulse after starting or adjusting a medication, tell us
about your caffeine intake.
A blood pressure measurement after an energy drink may tell us something very different from
your usual resting blood pressure.
Caffeine & Sleep
Caffeine can remain active in the body for hours.
You may be able to fall asleep after drinking coffee and still experience poorer sleep quality.
And caffeine isn't only in coffee.

Sleep: Your Brain Needs It More Than You Think
Sleep isn't simply the time when your body shuts down.
While you sleep, your brain is actively regulating systems involved in attention, memory,
emotion, stress, appetite, learning, and physical recovery.
That's why one of the questions you'll hear frequently at Ischys is:
"How are you sleeping?"
It's not small talk. Sleep gives us important information about your mental health.
When Sleep Changes, Everything Can Feel Harder
Too little sleep—or poor-quality sleep—can contribute to:
Difficulty concentrating
Forgetfulness
Irritability
Emotional reactivity
Anxiety
Low mood
Fatigue
Poor motivation
Increased appetite or cravings
Impulsive decisions
Reduced frustration tolerance
Sometimes these symptoms can look remarkably similar to psychiatric symptoms.
That doesn't mean mental health problems are "just poor sleep."
It means sleep and mental health influence each other.
Sleep & ADHD
ADHD and sleep problems frequently travel together.
Difficulty transitioning away from stimulating activities, losing track of time, racing thoughts,
delayed sleep schedules, and procrastinating bedtime can all interfere with sleep.
Then sleep deprivation can make the next day's:
attention • working memory • impulse control • emotional regulation • motivation
even more difficult.
This can create a cycle:
ADHD symptoms staying up late inadequate sleep worse executive functioning → → → →
harder day staying up late again →
If you're taking ADHD medication, sleep is also something we'll monitor when deciding whether
the medication, dose, and timing are working well for you.
Sleep, Depression & Anxiety
Depression can look very different from person to person.
Some people can't sleep.
Others sleep for long periods and still don't feel rested.
Anxiety can make the body tired while the brain remains wide awake.
You may recognize the experience:
"I'm exhausted, but my brain won't stop."
Improving sleep doesn't automatically cure depression or anxiety, but persistent sleep problems
can make recovery more difficult.
Sleep & Bipolar Disorder
Changes in sleep deserve particular attention for people with bipolar disorder.
Needing significantly less sleep without feeling tired can sometimes be an early sign of
hypomania or mania.
That's different from having insomnia and desperately wishing you could sleep.
If you have bipolar disorder and suddenly need much less sleep than usual while experiencing
increased energy, activity, impulsivity, irritability, or unusually elevated mood, contact your
mental health provider.
Sleep & Recovery
Sleep can be difficult during recovery from alcohol or other substances.
The brain and body may need time to re-establish normal sleep patterns, and using another
substance simply to force sleep can create additional problems.
If you're struggling with sleep during recovery, tell us.
Poor sleep is something to treat—not something you simply have to endure.
Your Body Has a Clock
Your brain has an internal timing system called the circadian rhythm.
Light is one of its strongest signals.
Morning light tells your brain:
"The day has started."
Darkness later helps signal:
"It's time to prepare for sleep."
This is why sleep isn't only about what you do during the 30 minutes before bedtime.
What you do in the morning and throughout the day matters too.
Five Things Worth Trying
1. Protect Your Wake-Up Time
A reasonably consistent wake time helps anchor your body's clock.
Sleeping dramatically later on days off can sometimes make returning to your normal schedule
more difficult.
You don't need perfection. Think consistency rather than rigidity.
2. Get Light in the Morning
Open the blinds.
Step outside.
Take the dog for a short walk.
Drink your morning coffee on the porch.
Natural outdoor light shortly after waking can help reinforce your circadian rhythm.
3. Watch Your Caffeine Timing
Caffeine can remain active in your system for hours.
If you're struggling with sleep, consider not only how much caffeine you consume, but when
you consume it.
Remember the caffeine page in this toolkit?
This is one reason it's here.
4. Give Your Brain a Landing Zone
Going directly from work, gaming, social media, homework, conflict, or intense television into bed
doesn't always give the nervous system much opportunity to transition.
Try creating a short wind-down routine.
It doesn't need to be elaborate.
Dim lights hygiene comfortable clothes quiet activity bed → → → →
Repeated routines can become cues that tell the brain what comes next.
5. Don't Turn Your Bed Into a Battleground
Lying in bed worrying about not sleeping can teach your brain that bed is where you struggle.
If you've been awake for a while and are becoming increasingly frustrated, sometimes getting up
briefly and doing something quiet in dim light until you feel sleepy is more helpful than fighting
with sleep.
What About Screens?
Phones aren't automatically evil.
The problem is often a combination of light, stimulation, emotional content, and losing track
of time.
"One more video" can become 45 minutes surprisingly easily.
Ask yourself:
Is my phone helping me wind down—or keeping my brain engaged?

Movement & Your Mental Health
You Don't Have to Become an Athlete
When people hear "exercise is good for your mental health," it can sound like one more thing
they're supposed to be doing.
That's not the point.
Movement can support mental health, and it doesn't have to mean joining a gym, running five
miles, or following a complicated workout program.
Walking counts. Dancing counts. Gardening counts. Playing with your kids counts.
Stretching counts.
The best movement is usually something you can actually imagine doing again.
Why Does Movement Help the Brain?
Physical activity affects much more than muscles.
Regular movement can influence systems involved in:
Mood • Stress • Sleep • Attention • Energy • Memory • Motivation • Physical Health
Exercise affects brain signaling and adaptation, including neurotransmitter systems and BDNF
(brain-derived neurotrophic factor), a protein involved in the brain's ability to adapt and learn.
Movement can also provide something psychologically important:
Evidence that you can do something difficult.
Sometimes that feeling of accomplishment matters almost as much as the exercise itself.
Movement & Depression
Depression often tells you:
"I don't have enough energy to exercise."
Unfortunately, doing less can lead to even less energy, less structure, fewer rewarding
experiences, and more isolation.
This is why movement can be part of behavioral activation.
The goal isn't:
"Exercise until you're not depressed."
The goal might simply be:
Get your body moving for 5 minutes.
Motivation doesn't always come first.
Sometimes:
Action accomplishment a little more energy more action → → →
Movement & ADHD
Movement can be especially useful for an ADHD brain.
Physical activity may temporarily support:
attention • executive functioning • mood regulation • restlessness • stress regulation
Try experimenting with movement before something that requires concentration.
For example:
10-minute walk homework →
Quick workout work project →
Walk the dog paperwork →
Pay attention to whether getting your body moving changes how easily your brain gets started.
Movement, Anxiety & Stress
When you're anxious, your nervous system may already feel prepared for action.
Your heart beats faster. Muscles tighten. You may feel restless or "keyed up."
Movement can give some of that physical activation somewhere to go.
For some people, walking, lifting weights, swimming, yoga, cycling, or other movement helps
reduce physical tension and makes it easier to settle afterward.
Different nervous systems respond differently.
Experiment and notice what yours prefers.
Movement & Recovery
Recovery isn't only about stopping a substance.
It can also involve rebuilding:
routine • reward • connection • confidence • physical health • identity
Movement can become part of that process.
A walking group, recreational sport, hiking club, gym class, or other shared activity can provide
both structure and connection.
It doesn't have to become another obsession or another way to punish yourself.
The goal is health—not perfection.

Food, Hydration & Your Brain
Your Brain Needs Fuel Too
Nutrition and mental health are connected—but that doesn't mean there's a perfect "mental
health diet."
Food isn't a cure for depression, ADHD, anxiety, or addiction, and you don't need to eat perfectly
for treatment to work.
What you eat and drink can, however, affect:
Energy • Concentration • Mood • Sleep • Medication Tolerance • Physical Health
At Ischys, we're interested in patterns—not perfection.
Start With the Basics
Before worrying about the newest supplement or superfood, ask:
Am I eating regularly?
Am I getting enough protein and nutrient-dense foods?
Am I drinking enough fluid?
Am I going most of the day without eating and then crashing later?
Sometimes the basics tell us more than we expect.
Your Brain Doesn't Like Running on Empty
Going long periods without eating can leave some people feeling:
Tired
Irritable
Shaky
Headachy
Unfocused
Anxious
Mentally foggy
Those sensations can sometimes be confused with psychiatric symptoms or medication side
effects.
This doesn't mean everyone needs to eat on exactly the same schedule.
It means notice how your body responds to yours.
Protein Matters
Protein provides amino acids that your body uses for many functions, including making signaling
molecules used throughout the brain and body.
Rather than worrying about calculating the perfect number of grams, consider whether you're
regularly including foods such as:
Eggs • Fish • Poultry • Meat • Dairy • Beans • Lentils • Tofu • Nuts • Seeds
If you take medication that decreases your appetite, eating something nutritious before your
appetite disappears may be easier than trying to eat later.
Plants Bring More Than Vitamins
Fruits, vegetables, beans, whole grains, nuts, and seeds provide combinations of:
Fiber • Vitamins • Minerals • Phytonutrients
Fiber also helps support the microorganisms living in the gastrointestinal tract—your gut
microbiome.
Researchers are actively studying the relationship between the gut microbiome and mental
health. The science is promising, but we're still learning exactly how much changing the
microbiome can improve specific psychiatric conditions.
So rather than chasing expensive "gut-brain" products:
Start with food.
Omega-3 Fatty Acids
Omega-3 fatty acids—particularly EPA and DHA—are important components of cell membranes
and have been studied extensively in brain and cardiovascular health.
Food sources include fatty fish such as:
Salmon • Sardines • Trout • Herring
Omega-3 supplements have also been studied for some psychiatric conditions, but supplement
formulations and evidence vary.
"Natural" doesn't automatically mean appropriate for everyone.
Talk with your healthcare provider before adding supplements, particularly if you take
medications or have medical conditions.
Hydration Counts
Even mild dehydration can make some people feel:
tired • headachy • dizzy • irritable • less focused
Water needs vary based on body size, activity, climate, pregnancy, medical conditions,
medications, and other factors.
Instead of forcing yourself to hit an arbitrary number:
Notice your pattern.
If you've had three coffees and an energy drink but almost no water today, that's useful
information.
ADHD & Eating
ADHD can interfere with nutrition in several ways.
You may:
Forget to eat.
Hyperfocus through meals.
Choose whatever requires the least preparation.
Seek highly rewarding foods when understimulated.
And some ADHD medications can reduce appetite.
If this sounds familiar, don't rely entirely on remembering to eat.
Try external supports:
alarms • prepared snacks • visible food • simple meals • eating before medication suppresses
appetite
Making healthy choices easier often works better than relying on willpower.
Depression & Food
When you're depressed, cooking can feel like an enormous task.
A nutritionally perfect meal that you don't have the energy to prepare isn't very helpful.

A sandwich.

Eggs and toast.
Soup.
A smoothie.
A simple frozen meal with something added to it.

Recovery & Nutrition
Substance use can disrupt appetite, eating patterns, sleep, gastrointestinal health, and overall
nutrition.
During recovery, hunger and cravings can also change.
Re-establishing regular meals can become part of rebuilding daily structure.
Nutrition doesn't replace treatment for substance use disorders—but physical recovery and
mental recovery aren't completely separate processes.

Appetite • Weight • Blood sugar • Cholesterol • Nausea • Constipation • Dry mouth

Fermented Foods & Your Gut Microbiome
Your digestive tract is home to trillions of microorganisms collectively known as the gut
microbiome.
Researchers are increasingly interested in the gut-brain axis—the two-way communication
system between the digestive tract and the brain. This communication involves the nervous
system, immune system, hormones, metabolism, and compounds produced by gut
microorganisms.
We're still learning how much changing the microbiome can directly influence specific mental
health conditions, so fermented foods shouldn't be considered a treatment for depression, anxiety,
ADHD, or other psychiatric disorders.
They can, however, be one part of a varied, nutrient-rich diet.
Consider Adding Fermented Foods
Fermentation can introduce live microorganisms into certain foods. Examples include:
Yogurt with live and active cultures
Kefir
Sauerkraut
Kimchi
Fermented vegetables
Some fermented or cultured drinks
Kombucha
Not every product marketed as "fermented" or "probiotic" contains meaningful amounts of live
microorganisms by the time you consume it, so check the label when that is your goal.
Don't Forget to Feed the Microbiome
Adding fermented foods is only part of the picture.
Many beneficial gut microorganisms use fiber and other fermentable carbohydrates from
foods such as:
Vegetables • Fruits • Beans • Lentils • Whole grains • Nuts • Seeds
Think of it this way:
Fermented foods may introduce microorganisms.
Fiber-rich plant foods help provide the environment and fuel for a diverse microbiome.
You don't need an expensive probiotic drink or supplement to begin supporting gut health.
Start With Food When You Can
Instead of immediately purchasing multiple probiotic supplements, consider experimenting with
ordinary foods.
Try adding:
Yogurt or kefir with breakfast
Sauerkraut alongside a meal
Kimchi with rice, eggs, vegetables, or another meal
A fermented food you genuinely enjoy
Then notice how you feel.
Some people experience temporary gas or bloating when significantly changing their intake of
fermented or high-fiber foods, so gradual changes may be more comfortable.
What About Kombucha and Fermented Drinks?
These can be enjoyable, but check the label.
Depending on the product, fermented drinks may contain varying amounts of:
Sugar • Caffeine • Alcohol
That can matter if you're monitoring blood sugar, caffeine intake, medications, sleep, pregnancy,
or substance-use recovery.
"Good for your gut" doesn't automatically mean unlimited amounts are better.

Connection: Your Brain Needs People Too
Mental Wellness Isn't Only an Inside Job
Humans are social creatures.
We don't all need the same amount of interaction—and being introverted isn't a problem—but
meaningful connection and belonging are important parts of mental and physical health.
When mental health becomes difficult, relationships are often one of the first things to change.
You stop answering texts.
You cancel plans.
You stay home.
You tell yourself:
"I'll see people when I feel better."
But sometimes isolation becomes part of what keeps us feeling poorly.
Connection Doesn't Mean Having Lots of Friends
You can be surrounded by people and still feel lonely.
And you can have only a few relationships and feel deeply connected.
What matters isn't simply how many people are around you.
Ask:
Do I have someone I can be myself with?
Do I feel understood?
Do I have someone I can contact when things aren't going well?
Do I feel like I belong somewhere?
Connection can come from:
Family • Friends • Partners • Neighbors • Coworkers • Faith communities • Support groups •
Clubs • Teams • Classes • Volunteering • Recovery communities • Online communities
Healthy connection can look different for different people.
Depression & Isolation
Depression often encourages withdrawal.
You may feel like:
"I'm not good company."
"I don't have the energy."
"Nobody wants to hear about my problems."
"I'll reach out when I'm feeling better."
The problem is that withdrawing can remove some of the very experiences that might help:
conversation • laughter • activity • perspective • affection • accountability • belonging
You don't need to become social overnight.
Sometimes the first step is simply being around another person.
ADHD & Relationships
ADHD can affect relationships in ways that have nothing to do with how much you care about
someone.
You may:
Forget to respond to messages
Lose track of time
Interrupt
Forget plans
Become distracted during conversations
Hyperfocus and disappear into an activity
Feel overwhelmed by maintaining friendships
Experience intense emotions around perceived criticism or rejection
Instead of assuming:
"I'm terrible at relationships,"
try asking:
"What support would make staying connected easier?"
Calendar reminders, scheduled calls, shared activities, recurring plans, or simply explaining your
ADHD to people you trust can sometimes help.
Anxiety Can Make Your World Smaller
Avoidance works incredibly well in the short term.
If a social situation makes you anxious and you cancel, your anxiety usually drops.
Your brain learns:
Avoiding it made me feel better.
That can make avoidance more likely next time.
Over time, however, your world can become smaller.
When appropriate, gradually approaching safe situations rather than repeatedly avoiding them
can help your brain learn:
"I can tolerate this."
Recovery Needs Connection
Substance use disorders thrive in isolation.
Recovery often requires building—or rebuilding—a life that provides:
support • accountability • belonging • purpose • sober activities • people to contact when
things become difficult
That might include:
Family or friends

12-step programs
SMART Recovery
Therapy or group therapy
Faith or community groups
Recreational activities
There isn't one correct recovery community.
The important question is:
"Who helps me move toward the life I want?"
Not Every Relationship Is Healthy
Connection doesn't mean tolerating relationships that repeatedly harm you.
Healthy relationships generally allow room for:
Respect • Boundaries • Safety • Honesty • Individuality • Repair after conflict
Sometimes improving mental health involves strengthening relationships.
Sometimes it involves setting boundaries.
And sometimes it means creating distance from relationships that are unsafe or consistently
destructive.

Stress & Your Nervous System
When Your Brain Knows You're Safe—but Your Body Doesn't Feel That Way
Stress isn't only something you think about.
You can feel it throughout your body:
Racing heart • Tight muscles • Shallow breathing • Upset stomach • Sweating • Restlessness •
Irritability • Shaking • Feeling overwhelmed
Sometimes you know logically that you're okay, but your body hasn't received the message yet.
Learning how to influence that physical response can become another tool in your mental-health
toolbox.
Your Nervous System Has Different Gears
When your brain detects stress or threat, your body prepares to respond.
You may move toward a more activated state:
FIGHT / FLIGHT
You might feel:
Anxious • Panicky • Angry • Restless • Irritable • Overstimulated • Unable to sit still
Other times, overwhelming stress can look more like:
SHUTDOWN / FREEZE
You might feel:
Numb • Disconnected • Exhausted • Foggy • Unmotivated • "Not really here"
Neither response means you're weak.
These are protective responses of the nervous system.
The goal isn't to never experience them.
The goal is to develop more ways to help yourself return toward a manageable state.
First: Name What's Happening
Before trying to fix the feeling, pause.
What am I noticing right now?
My body: _______________________________________
My thoughts: ____________________________________
My emotion: _____________________________________
Then ask:
Do I need to CALM my system—or ACTIVATE it?
That's important because the same coping strategy doesn't work for every state.
If You're Anxious, Panicky, Angry or Overstimulated
Try something that reduces activation.
Slow Your Exhale
You don't need a complicated breathing technique.
Try:
Breathe in gently.
Breathe out slowly and a little longer than you breathed in.
Repeat several times.
Don't force enormous breaths. For some people, repeatedly taking very deep breaths can actually
increase lightheadedness.
Think:
Slow and gentle—not huge.
Ground Yourself in the Present
When thoughts are racing, deliberately direct your attention outward.
Try the 5-4-3-2-1 technique:
5 things you can see
4 things you can feel
3 things you can hear
2 things you can smell
1 thing you can taste
The goal isn't to magically eliminate anxiety.
It's to give your attention somewhere else to go.
Use Temperature
A cool washcloth on your face, cool water, stepping outside, or another safe temperature change
can sometimes interrupt escalating physical distress.
Notice whether your body responds.
Move the Activation
Sometimes your body doesn't want another breathing exercise.
It wants to move.
Try:
Walking • Stretching • Dancing • Cleaning • Exercising • Shaking out your hands • Going
outside
Especially when you're restless or angry, movement may be more useful than trying to force
yourself to sit perfectly still.
If You're Shut Down, Numb or Stuck
Relaxation may not be what you need.
You may need gentle activation.
Try:
Stand up.
Open the blinds.
Turn on music.
Take a shower.
Step outside.
Walk for five minutes.
Call another person.
Change rooms.
Do one small physical task.
The goal isn't:
"Become productive."
The goal is:
Change your state a little.
Give Your Brain Something Concrete
When everything feels overwhelming, reduce the size of the problem.
Instead of:
"I need to clean my house."
Try:
"Put five things away."
Instead of:
"I have to finish this project."
Try:
"Open the document."
Instead of:
"I need to get my life together."
Try:
"What is the next physical action?"
This can be particularly useful when stress combines with ADHD, depression, or anxiety.
Use Your Senses
Your senses can help anchor attention.
Experiment with:
Music
A warm shower
A weighted blanket
A familiar scent
Holding something textured
Going outside
Petting an animal
Stretching
A warm or cold drink
Different people respond differently.
Find what works for your nervous system.
Connection Can Regulate Us Too
Sometimes the best coping skill is another human being.
Call someone.
Sit next to someone.
Ask for a hug if that feels comfortable.
Walk with someone.
Tell someone:
"I'm having a rough moment. I don't need you to fix it—I just don't want to be alone with it."
Humans often regulate better with safe connection than in complete isolation.
What About Cravings?
A craving is an experience—not a command.
Cravings often rise, peak, and eventually decrease.
Instead of immediately acting on the urge:
Create space between the urge and the action.
Try:
Delay.
Change locations.
Contact someone.
Go outside.
Eat something if you're hungry.
Move your body.
Attend a recovery meeting.
Remove access when possible.
You don't have to promise yourself:
"Never again."
Sometimes the immediate goal is simply:
"Not right now."

These don't explain every difficult emotion or craving.
But they're useful places to start.

Substances & Your Mental Health
No Judgment. Just Better Information.
Alcohol, cannabis, nicotine, prescription medications, and other substances can affect mood,
anxiety, attention, sleep, motivation, heart rate, blood pressure, and how medications feel
or work.
That's why we'll ask about them.
Not to judge you.
Not to get you in trouble.
We need an accurate picture of everything your brain and body are being exposed to so we can
make safer, better-informed treatment decisions.
Why It Matters
A substance may seem to help one symptom in the short term while making another problem
worse later.
For example:
Alcohol may make you sleepy but disrupt sleep later in the night. →
Nicotine may briefly improve alertness but withdrawal between doses can contribute to →
irritability, anxiety, or difficulty concentrating.
Cannabis may feel relaxing but for some people it can worsen anxiety, memory, →
motivation, attention, or psychiatric symptoms.
Stimulant or recreational substances may increase energy while also affecting sleep, →
anxiety, heart rate, and blood pressure.
This doesn't mean every person responds the same way.
That's exactly why we ask.
Alcohol
Alcohol is a central nervous system depressant, even though some people initially feel more
social, relaxed, or energized after drinking.
Alcohol can affect:
Sleep • Mood • Anxiety • Judgment • Coordination • Memory • Impulse control
It can also interact with psychiatric medications.
Combining alcohol with medications or substances that cause sedation can be particularly risky.
If alcohol is part of your life, tell us how much and how often rather than simply saying
"occasionally."
That information helps.
Cannabis
People use cannabis for many reasons:
Sleep • Anxiety • Pain • Relaxation • Appetite • Recreation • Trauma symptoms
Some people perceive benefits.
Others experience:
Anxiety • Panic • Paranoia • Memory problems • Reduced concentration • Changes in
motivation • Sleep problems
Cannabis products also vary substantially in THC concentration, formulation, and frequency of
use, so "I use marijuana" doesn't tell us everything we need to know.
If you use cannabis, we'd rather know.
Nicotine & Vaping
Nicotine is a stimulant and is highly reinforcing.
It can temporarily affect:
Alertness • Attention • Mood • Heart rate • Blood pressure
When nicotine levels fall, withdrawal can cause:
Irritability • Restlessness • Anxiety • Difficulty concentrating • Cravings
That can create an important clinical question:
"Is my ADHD/anxiety getting worse—or am I experiencing nicotine withdrawal?"
Sometimes it's both.
Vapes and nicotine pouches can also make it surprisingly easy to consume nicotine repeatedly
throughout the day.
Prescription Medications Can Matter Too
Taking a medication exactly as prescribed is different from misusing it—but we still need the full
picture.
Tell us about medications including:
Pain medications • Benzodiazepines • Sleep medications • Stimulants • Muscle relaxants •
Weight-loss medications • Other controlled medications
Also tell us about medications prescribed by other providers.
Your psychiatric medication doesn't exist in isolation from everything else you're taking.
Other Substances
Please tell us about other substances too, including things that may feel uncomfortable to discuss.
Our priority is safety and effective treatment.
Knowing about substance use can change decisions involving:
Medication selection • Dosing • Drug interactions • Withdrawal risk • Overdose risk •
Cardiovascular monitoring • Sleep treatment • ADHD treatment
Accurate information helps us protect you.
Mixing Substances Can Change the Risk
One substance may be relatively predictable by itself but become substantially more dangerous
when combined with another.
Of particular concern is combining multiple substances or medications that slow the central
nervous system and breathing.
If you're unsure whether something you're taking is safe with your medication, ask.
That's a good question—not an inconvenience.
Recovery Isn't All-or-Nothing
If you're trying to reduce or stop substance use, progress may not follow a perfectly straight line.
A return to use doesn't erase everything you've learned.
Instead ask:
What happened before it?
What was I feeling?
Who was I with?
What need was I trying to meet?
What could make the next situation safer?
Understanding the pattern gives us something to work with.
Be Curious About the Job the Substance Is Doing
Instead of only asking:
"How do I stop?"
also ask:
"What is this doing for me?"
Maybe it helps you:
Fall asleep * Feel less anxious
Concentrate * Feel confident socially
Escape painful emotions * Have energy
Feel less bored * Manage pain
Feel something * Feel nothing
Connect with other people
If we understand the function, we can work on finding safer ways to meet the same need.

Getting the Most From Your Medication
Medication Is a Tool—Not the Whole Toolbox
Psychiatric medication can be an important part of treatment.
But medication isn't meant to change who you are.
The goal is to reduce symptoms that interfere with your ability to function, connect, think,
sleep, work, learn, recover, and enjoy your life.
At Ischys, medication decisions are something we make with you, not simply for you.
Know Why You're Taking It
Before starting a medication, you should understand:
1. Why am I taking this?
2. What symptoms are we hoping it will improve?
3. When might I notice a difference?
4. What common side effects should I watch for?
5. What symptoms mean I should contact my provider?
If you don't know the answers, ask.
Understanding your treatment is part of your treatment.
How Do We Know Whether It's Working?
"Do you think the medication is working?"
Sometimes that's surprisingly difficult to answer.
Instead, look for specific changes.
Maybe you're:
Getting out of bed more easily.
Finishing tasks.
Interrupting less.
Having fewer panic attacks.
Sleeping through the night.
Feeling less irritable.
Experiencing fewer cravings.
Getting assignments completed.
Enjoying things again.
Handling frustration differently.
Those changes often tell us more than simply saying:
"I think I feel better."
Some Medications Take Time
Not every psychiatric medication works immediately.
Some effects may appear relatively quickly, while others can take days or several weeks to
become clear.
We may also deliberately start with a lower dose and increase gradually.
Why?
Because we're balancing:
Benefit + Tolerability + Safety
Starting with more medication isn't automatically better.
Our goal is to find an appropriate dose for you.
Side Effects Are Information
If you experience a side effect, tell us.
Don't assume:
"I just have to tolerate this."
And don't automatically stop the medication without discussing it with your provider unless
you've been given specific instructions to do so or you're experiencing an emergency.
Sometimes a side effect can be addressed by:
Changing the dose • Changing timing • Slowing titration • Treating the side effect • Changing
medications
We can't help troubleshoot something we don't know about.
Timing Matters
Taking medication inconsistently can make it difficult to determine whether it's helping.
If remembering medication is difficult, consider:
Phone alarms
Pill organizers
Medication apps
Pairing medication with an existing routine
Keeping medication somewhere appropriate and visible
If ADHD makes remembering your ADHD medication difficult...
You're definitely not the first person that's happened to.
Let's build a system instead of relying entirely on memory.
Don't Forget Everything Else You're Taking
Tell us about:
Prescription medications Over-the-counter medications Vitamins
Herbs Supplements Pre-workout products
Energy products Cannabis Alcohol
Other substances
"Natural" does not automatically mean interaction-free.
Some supplements can affect serotonin, sedation, blood pressure, bleeding, liver enzymes, or the
way medications are metabolized.
Bring us the whole picture.
Caffeine Counts Too
Remember the caffeine section?
Caffeine can affect:
Sleep • Anxiety • Heart rate • Blood pressure • Tremor • Restlessness
This can become particularly important when you're taking medications that affect some of the
same systems.
If you suddenly increase from one cup of coffee to several energy drinks per day, tell us.
That information matters.
Your Body Gives Us Data
Depending on your medication, we may monitor things such as:
Blood pressure • Pulse • Weight • Sleep • Appetite • Laboratory values • Movement symptoms
• Mood changes
Monitoring doesn't necessarily mean something is wrong.
It's part of prescribing responsibly.
Tell Us About Major Changes
Please let your provider know about important changes such as:
Pregnancy or plans for pregnancy
Breastfeeding
Major medical diagnoses
New medications
Hospitalizations
Significant changes in substance use
Major changes in sleep
New or significantly worsening psychiatric symptoms
These can affect medication decisions.
Don't Chase Someone Else's Dose
Your friend takes 20 mg.
Someone online takes 100 mg.
Another person says a medication was "life-changing."
Someone else says it was terrible.
None of that tells us what your response will be.
Medication response can vary based on:
Diagnosis • Genetics • Metabolism • Other medications • Medical conditions • Age • Sleep •
Substance use • Individual biology
The goal isn't to reach a particular number.
The goal is the lowest appropriate dose that provides meaningful benefit with acceptable
side effects.

The more accurate the information, the better decisions we can make together.

Make Your World Smaller
When your brain says:
"I can't handle everything."
It may be correct.
So don't handle everything.
Ask:
What is the next small thing?
Not:
"Clean the house."
Try:
Put five things away.
Not:
"Catch up on work."
Try:
Open the email.
Not:
"Get healthy."
Try:
Drink a glass of water.
Not:
"Fix my life."
Try:
Take the next useful action.
Change Something Physical
When thoughts are stuck, change something around your body.
Try one:
Stand up.
Go outside.
Change rooms.
Take a shower.
Turn on music.
Stretch.
Walk for five minutes.
Eat something.
Open the blinds.
Put your phone down.
Sometimes changing the environment helps interrupt the loop.
Don't Believe Every Thought You Have on a Bad Day
A difficult emotional state can change how you interpret yourself, other people, and your future.
Thoughts such as:
"Nothing is going to change."
"Everyone is disappointed in me."
"I've ruined everything."
"I'm never going to get better."
can feel completely true in the moment.
Before treating the thought as a fact, ask:
"Would I see this the same way on one of my better days?"
You don't have to force yourself to think positively.
Just leave room for the possibility that your current state is influencing the story your brain is
telling you.
Delay Major Decisions
When possible, a highly emotional moment may not be the best time to:
Quit your job.
End an important relationship.
Send the angry message.
Drop out of school.
Make a major purchase.
Stop treatment.
Throw away your medication.
Write the thought down.
Talk with someone you trust.
Sleep on it when it is safe to do so.
You can still make the decision later.
If You're Overwhelmed
Try:
STOP BREATHE CHOOSE ONE THING → →
Ask:
What actually needs my attention today?
What can wait?
What can someone else help with?
What am I expecting from myself that isn't realistic today?
A reduced-capacity day may require a reduced-capacity plan.
If You're Craving
Don't argue with yourself for the next six months.
Focus on the next few minutes.
Delay the action.
Then:
Change location.
Contact someone.
Eat if you're hungry.
Drink water.
Move your body.
Attend a recovery meeting.
Remove access when possible.
Use your recovery plan.
Remember:
A craving is an experience. It isn't an instruction.
If You're Isolating
Depression, anxiety, shame, and substance use can all encourage isolation.
Try sending one message:
"I'm having a rough day. Can you talk for a few minutes?"
You don't need to explain everything.
Sometimes another person simply being present changes the moment.

When a Hard Day Becomes a Crisis
There is a difference between having a difficult day and being unable to keep yourself or someone
else safe.
If you are experiencing suicidal thoughts, thoughts of seriously harming yourself or another
person, a potentially life-threatening overdose, severe intoxication, or another immediate
safety emergency, get help now.
Call or text 988
The 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline provides crisis support in the United States.
Call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department
for an immediate medical or safety emergency.
If you're experiencing a substance-related crisis or believe an overdose may have occurred, seek
emergency medical assistance.
You do not need to wait for your next appointment when something is urgent.
